The Store Register

What the person on duty actually sees — receiving stock, issuing it, taking it back — and why the screens are shaped this way.

The person on duty never types a number from memory, and never types an identifier.

Every quantity and every person comes off a list the software puts in front of them. That single rule is what makes the register survive a shift change — because the software remembers, not the person at the desk.

The handwritten book failed in one specific way: it put the arithmetic and the memory inside people's heads. Whoever was standing there had to know how many chairs were left, and had to trust that the man returning forty chairs had taken forty. A different person each shift means that knowledge walks out of the door at 6pm.

So every screen below is built to answer a question before it is asked.

On arrival

Who is on duty?

One question, once, at the start of a shift. Nobody types their own name forty times a day, and every entry made afterwards is stamped with it automatically — so Person Incharge fills itself in and can never be left blank or misspelt.

Home

The screen that replaces the shift handover

This is what is open all day. The new person walking in at 2pm reads it and knows everything the previous person knew — what is on hand, and what is out with whom.

The outstanding list sits at the same weight as the stock list on purpose. Stock counts survive a handover by themselves; the knowledge of who is still holding forty chairs is exactly what used to get lost.

Event 1

500 chairs arrive at the gate

Suresh presses Stuff came in. The form is the left half of the old book page, in the same order, with the two things you asked for that the paper never had: who it came from, and who took delivery.

The product name is the dangerous field. If Monday's person types “Chairs” and Wednesday's types “chair”, you now own two products, your stock count is wrong, and there is no fixing it mid-event. So the name is chosen from a list — and creating a brand-new product is a separate, deliberate press, never something that happens by accident because a word was misspelt.

Event 2

Ravi comes to take 10 chairs

A different person is at the desk now — Anita, who started at 2pm and has never met Ravi. She does not need to know anything.

The quantity box carries the answer to “do we even have these?” right beside it, and refuses a number larger than what is on hand. Nobody can issue 900 chairs out of 890 by mistyping.

Event 3

Someone comes to return chairs

This is the case you were most worried about — how does the person on duty know the man is returning the right number? The answer is that they are never asked to know it.

Imran, on duty at 6pm, searches by name, mobile or department. Up comes everything that person is still holding. He picks the line, and the return quantity is already filled in with the full amount outstanding. He only changes it if fewer come back — and the moment he does, the shortfall is stated in plain words on the screen.

Returning more than was taken is refused outright. And because the man handing things back is not always the man who took them, the person returning is recorded separately.

Nothing disappears quietly

Those 5 chairs are not written off. They stay against Ravi’s name with the remark attached, and they stay inside the 490 chairs still owed to Sharma. The only thing the software learned is that waiting is no longer the plan — someone will have to pay for them.

End of event

What is owed back to each supplier

Rented things have to go back to whoever they came from. Because stock is pooled — we agreed a chair is a chair, no matter which truck it arrived on — this view works at the level that actually matters: how many did we take from each supplier, and how many have gone back.

It is a list to read, nothing more. There is no button here.

Lines that never reach zero, and that is fine

Sharma is owed 490 chairs, 5 of which are broken and will never arrive. That line stays open. Whatever is worked out with Mr. Sharma happens over a phone call, not in here — the software’s job is to state the true number, not to close the argument.

If the day comes that it should read zero, someone records the 5 as received and it does. No separate machinery, no money screen. If that turns out to be too crude in practice, we build something then.

Agents

The four that will build this

Both collections were searched before anything was written, as CLAUDE.md requires — around 550 agent definitions between them. The honest result: most are pitched at enterprise work and over-fit this job. Two were worth adapting, two had to be written, and the rest were left alone.

spec-writercct · sdd-spec-writerAdaptedTurns this page into a build contract so the implementation can’t drift. Had no frontmatter at all, so the when-to-use description and read-only-plus-write tools are ours. Test cases must carry real numbers — 890 chairs, not foo.
release-gateaa · testing-reality-checkerAdaptedRefuses to certify anything without evidence it gathered itself. Persona sections stripped per CLAUDE.md, and the automatic-fail list rewritten around this project: the cross-compile, the atomic-write path, the stock arithmetic, and binding to loopback.

Ours carries the constraints that actually bind: the cgo-free Windows cross-compile, standard library first, loopback only, and atomic writes with a .bak fallback.
plain-language-reviewer— nothing —NewA search of both collections for microcopy and plain-language roles returned only documentation agents — technical-writer, documentation-expert — which write API references and user guides. None reviews the words on a button. For software run by people who were never trained on it, that is the review that matters most.

Looked at and left

desktop-app-engineer is Electron, Tauri, code signing and auto-update — all four already rejected. backend-architect designs microservice boundaries and Kafka topics; there is one binary and one file. design-persona-walkthrough has exactly the right method — walk each screen as the person using it — but is framed for marketing conversion, so the method was borrowed into this page and the file left where it was.

Decided

Things that don’t come back

What happens when something never comes back

A chair breaks. A drum walks off. Nothing gets written off quietly: the missing pieces stay against the name of whoever had them, carrying a remark in plain words saying what happened — the margin scribble from the old book, given a proper home.

They also stay inside what is owed to the supplier, and they stay there. The register keeps showing 490 owed and does not pretend otherwise — whatever gets worked out with Mr. Sharma is a conversation between two people, and the paper is allowed to sit unsettled while it happens.

The one thing the person on duty chooses is whether the missing pieces are still expected back or definitely gone. That is the only part of it a computer cannot work out on its own.
